## Article structure

**Working title:** All Things Ceiling Fans: Sizing, Styles, Winter Benefits & the New LED Options

1. **Why a ceiling fan still matters even with aircon in the house**
   - A fan cools *people*, not the room — the wind-chill effect on skin is what makes a space feel cooler, not an actual temperature drop, which is why running a fan can't replace aircon in genuinely hot conditions but is a much cheaper way to feel comfortable most of the time
   - Running a fan alongside aircon and raising the thermostat a couple of degrees is a real, practical energy-saving combination, not just a fan-only alternative
   - **Chris's own home is the example:** fans in the bedrooms are practically all that's needed there — used consistently for around 3 months of the year with no aircon required in those rooms at all. Good, credible real-world proof point rather than a generic efficiency claim.

2. **Sizing — matching fan diameter to room size**
   - Under 7m²: 90–107cm
   - 7–13m²: 107–122cm
   - 13–20m²: 122–132cm
   - 20–27m²: 132–142cm
   - 27m²+: 142cm+ or consider two fans rather than one oversized unit
   - Extra-large fans (up to 2m+): a genuine option for big open-plan living or alfresco spaces, and often a better result than multiple smaller fans fighting each other's airflow
   - **[NEEDS CHRIS]** — his own rules of thumb if they differ from the general guide above, and when he'd choose two smaller fans over one large one

3. **Mounting styles and ceiling height — this is where a lot of buying mistakes happen**
   - Australian wiring rules set a hard minimum: fan blades must clear at least 2.1m from the floor — non-negotiable, not a style preference
   - **Standard downrod fans**: more drop from the ceiling generally means better airflow — the preferred option whenever ceiling height allows it
   - **Low-profile/hugger (flush-mount) fans**: sit close to the ceiling, needed whenever a standard downrod would push blades below the 2.1m minimum — typically required below roughly 2.4m ceilings. Worth being upfront in the article that a hugger fan is a compromise for clearance, not the first choice on airflow grounds
   - Downrod length rule of thumb: roughly ceiling height minus 2.4m gives a sensible drop, landing blades around the optimal 2.1–2.7m height band
   - **Chris's preferred bedroom height:** around 2400mm FFL (floor-to-fitting) — chosen specifically so the airflow effect is genuinely felt even on the lowest, quietest speed, rather than needing a faster/noisier setting to feel the fan working. Worth including this as a practical target height, not just an abstract formula.

4. **The winter benefit almost nobody uses — destratification**
   - Warm air rises and pools near the ceiling, especially in high-ceiling rooms — this is exactly the "necessary thing" Chris flagged: a high-ceiling space without a fan is often quietly wasting a layer of warm air sitting uselessly overhead
   - Running the fan in reverse (clockwise when viewed from below) at **low speed** gently mixes that pooled warm air back down along the walls, without creating a direct cooling draft — the goal is redistribution, not a breeze, which is why speed matters here
   - Can meaningfully reduce heating reliance in a high-ceiling room, particularly relevant for the kind of open-plan/raked-ceiling living spaces that are common in newer builds
   - **Resolved:** no specific client before/after feedback to feature — present this section on the mechanism/principle alone rather than a case study

5. **AC vs DC motor — the efficiency conversation most buyers skip**
   - DC motor fans can be up to ~70% more efficient than AC equivalents — roughly 30W vs 100W for comparable airflow
   - DC also typically means quieter operation, more speed steps (finer control than old-style AC 3-speed), and remote/smart control as standard rather than an add-on
   - Higher upfront cost, but often a 15–20 year practical lifespan — worth framing as a genuine long-term value conversation, not just an upsell
   - **Chris's current default:** almost all models he specifies now are DC — quieter, reverse function built into the remote, and they simply look better than older AC units. Worth stating this plainly as the current baseline recommendation rather than presenting AC vs DC as a genuinely open choice — DC has essentially won for Chris's purposes, with AC only relevant as a budget/legacy option.

6. **The new integrated LED options — genuinely practical now, not a gimmick**
   - Modern integrated LED light kits are slim enough to almost disappear against a white fan body when switched off — a real change from the bulky light kits of a few years ago
   - Dimmable as standard on most current options, and CCT-selectable is now common across mainstream ranges — typically a tri-tone toggle (roughly 3000K/4500K/6000K) rather than fully tunable, letting one fitting serve as both a warm ambient light and a brighter functional one
   - Some premium models now also offer colour-changing capability, though worth framing honestly as a nice-to-have rather than the main selling point of an integrated fan light
   - Ties directly to the site's existing CCT/CRI content — worth a cross-link on how to actually choose the CCT setting for the room
   - **Chris's real take — integrated vs separate is a genuine consultation question, not a simple rule:** integrated fan lights can be ideal for bedrooms depending on room size — a decent integrated fan light is often enough for a bedroom, especially a kid's room, and means not having to spec a full separate downlight layout as well. Worth presenting this honestly as "often good enough, sometimes not" rather than picking a side.
   - **Callout — Chris's favourite master bedroom approach:** two uplights and no ceiling light at all (which also means no fan-light combo in that room). This works specifically when there's no exposed wardrobe/robe in the room itself — i.e., storage is in a separate walk-in robe (WIR) rather than built-in robes needing their own light coverage. If there are robes in the room, they need their own lighting regardless of this approach. Worth a dedicated small callout box — it's a distinctive, opinionated design choice that's genuinely interesting content, not just a fan spec detail.

7. **Outdoor and alfresco fans — matching the rating to real exposure**
   - **Damp-rated**: built for covered outdoor areas with no direct rain exposure — patios, verandas, alfresco areas with solid cover
   - **Wet-rated (typically IP44)**: sealed motor housing, genuinely built to handle direct rain exposure — needed for open balconies, poolside, or any area rain can actually reach
   - **Chris's current recommendation — go further than IP44 where possible:** full IP65-rated fans now exist and are his preference for outdoor use — better protection against sideways-driven rain and dust/dirt ingress, not just splashing water from any direction like IP44 covers. Worth positioning IP65 as the "do it properly" recommendation rather than treating IP44 as the ceiling of what's available.
   - The mistake to call out plainly: using a damp-rated fan in a genuinely exposed spot because it "looked outdoor enough" — same underlying lesson as the site's exterior fitting-material content, applied to fans specifically

8. **The strobe trap — lighting positioned near a spinning fan (new section, from Chris's notes)**
   - A genuinely advanced, non-obvious point worth its own section: light fittings situated above or near a ceiling fan can interact with the rotating blades to create a **stroboscopic effect** — a visible flicker/strobe as blades repeatedly pass through the light path, which can range from mildly distracting to genuinely unpleasant depending on blade speed and the light source's own flicker characteristics
   - This connects directly to the flicker-sensitivity content already published in the CCT/CRI/beam-angle guide's physiology section — a poor-quality driver with inherent flicker is more likely to produce a noticeable strobe effect when combined with fan blade movement than a genuinely flicker-free one
   - Practical framing: this needs to be worked out carefully at the design stage — fitting position relative to the fan, and driver/light quality, both matter here. Not a reason to avoid combining fans and nearby lighting, but a real trap worth naming so it doesn't get discovered after installation
   - **[NEEDS CHRIS]** — any real examples of this being an issue on a job, and what he did to fix or avoid it (reposition the fitting? change fan speed? swap the driver?) — would make this section land much harder with a concrete example

9. **Practical takeaway box** — a short decision path: measure the room and check ceiling height first (clearance is non-negotiable) → pick downrod vs hugger based on that (DC as the default motor choice) → confirm IP65 rather than just IP44 for genuine outdoor exposure → check fitting positions near the fan for strobe risk before finalising the layout
