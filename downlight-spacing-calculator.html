💡 Quick Facts

Glare risk

Wide beam angles (≥60°) at low ceiling heights (≤2.4m) throw more light toward eye level. Consider a narrower beam or an anti-glare trim near seating and TV walls.

The "cave effect"

Anti-glare downlights concentrate light straight down with little spill to the walls. Floor lux can read fine while the room still feels flat — because perceived brightness depends heavily on wall illumination.

📐 Understanding Downlight Spacing

What is downlight spacing?

Downlight spacing is the centre-to-centre distance between fixtures. Get it right and light pools overlap enough for an even wash across the room; too far apart and you get dark patches between fittings, too close and you're paying for fixtures you don't need.

Beam angle drives the maths

A downlight's beam angle and mounting height determine the diameter of its light cone at the target plane. Recommended spacing is a fraction of that diameter — tighter for an even wash, wider for defined feature pools.

Wall offset matters too

Fixtures placed a full spacing-width from the wall often leave a dark perimeter strip, especially with anti-glare fittings. A tighter offset (~0.4× spacing) keeps the edges of the room from feeling flat.

Room type changes the answer

Living areas usually want anti-glare fittings for comfort. Bathrooms, laundries and utility rooms are low-dwell spaces where a frosted/opal diffuser's wider spread is a better trade-off than its slightly higher glare.

Spacing isn't the same as brightness

Beam angle and spacing control how evenly light is spread — they say nothing about how bright the room actually is. Two fixtures with the same beam angle but different lumen output need different spacing (or different fixture counts) to hit the same target lux. Fill in fixture lumens above to check.

🧮 Formulas & Method

Even Coverage method — the standard beam-angle-cone simplification found across consumer lighting calculators. It spaces fixtures purely so their beam pools overlap evenly, regardless of how bright the result is:

Cone diameter: D = 2 × H × tan(θ / 2)
Recommended spacing: S = D × k  (k = overlap factor, 0.6–1.0)
Wall offset: S / 2  (tightened to ~0.4 × S for anti-glare fixtures)
Fixture count: rows × columns fitted to room length × width

H = ceiling height minus target plane height, θ = beam angle in degrees. This method alone tends to over-specify residential rooms — a narrow-beam fixture "needs" a tight grid for a seamless wash even when far fewer fixtures would light the room perfectly well.

Residential method (the default) — works backwards from the Lumen Method instead, the same formula behind the Lux Calculator, to find the fewest fixtures that hit your target lux:

Fixture count: N = ⌈(target lux × A) / (Φ × UF × MF)⌉
Achieved lux: Em = (N × Φ × UF × MF) / A

Φ = lumens per fixture, MF = maintenance factor (0.80), A = room area (m²). UF (utilisation factor) starts from the fixture's optical type — anti-glare/deep-baffle (0.55) keeps light in a tight column with little reaching the walls, standard reflector (0.65) is the average-room baseline, frosted/opal diffuser (0.75) spreads further and lifts overall room utilisation — then adjusts for Room Index (K), the same measure the Lux Calculator uses: K = (L × W) / (H × (L + W)). For a fixed room footprint K is exactly proportional to 1/H, so the height adjustment reduces to (2.4m / H)0.4 against a 2.4m reference height (about +8% UF at 2.0m, −18% at 4.0m) — a taller ceiling for the room's size genuinely needs more fixtures for the same lux target, a lower one needs fewer. Beam angle still only affects the Even Coverage method and the beam cone diameter D, not this formula — it changes how evenly a given fixture count lights the room, not how much total light is needed.

N is then arranged into a grid matching the room's proportions. Because this can space fixtures further apart than their beam actually reaches, the calculator flags it when the resulting spacing exceeds the beam cone diameter D — a sign you'll see pooling rather than a perfectly even wash, which is a normal, common trade-off in homes.

A note on accuracy: both methods are practical estimates, not photometric calculations. The rigorous method uses a fixture's actual spacing-to-height ratio (SHR) from its manufacturer's IES/photometric file, and should take precedence where one is published. AS/NZS 1680 targets a uniformity ratio (Emin/Eavg) of at least 0.4 for general areas — the Lux Calculator's Room Grid mode gives a more detailed brightness check once you've settled on a layout here.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

How far apart should downlights be? ▼
It depends on beam angle and ceiling height. As a rule of thumb, spacing should be roughly equal to the mounting height for a standard flood beam, tighter for narrow spot fixtures and wider for wide flood fixtures. Use the calculator above for a figure matched to your exact fixture and room.
How close to the wall should downlights be? ▼
A common starting point is half the recommended spacing. With anti-glare/deep-baffle fixtures, tightening this to around 0.4× the spacing helps avoid a dark strip along the walls, since these fittings throw little light sideways.
What beam angle should I use for downlights? ▼
Narrow beams (24°) suit feature lighting and accent pools. Medium beams (36–38°) are the most common general-purpose choice for living areas. Wide beams (60°) give an even wash but increase glare risk at low ceiling heights.
Does fixture wattage or lumen output change how many downlights I need? ▼
Yes — this is exactly why Auto mode exists. Beam-angle spacing alone (Even Coverage) sizes fixtures for a perfectly seamless wash and can massively over-specify a home — e.g. a 4×4m room with a narrow 36° beam works out to 16 fixtures that way. Sizing to a lumen output and target lux level instead can bring that down to as few as 6 fixtures for the same room — the number you'd actually install. Auto picks whichever method needs fewer fixtures automatically.
Does a frosted diffuser lens really need fewer downlights than a clear anti-glare one? ▼
Yes, for a real reason beyond comfort. An anti-glare/deep-baffle fixture keeps almost all its output in a tight column straight down, so very little reaches the walls or ceiling. A frosted/opal diffuser spreads its output much further, which raises the room's overall utilisation factor (UF) — the same UF concept the Lux Calculator uses. Higher UF means more of each fixture's lumens are doing useful work, so fewer fixtures are needed for the same target lux. The calculator uses UF 0.55 for anti-glare, 0.65 for standard reflector, and 0.75 for frosted/opal — change the Fixture Optical Type field above and the fixture count updates to match.
Why does my room feel dark even though I have enough downlights? ▼
This is usually the "cave effect" — anti-glare downlights direct almost all their light straight down, so walls stay comparatively dark even when the floor reads a good lux level. Perceived brightness depends heavily on wall illumination. Frosted/opal fixtures or added wall lighting usually fix it.
Should I use frosted or clear downlights in a bathroom? ▼
Frosted/opal diffusers are usually the better fit for bathrooms, laundries and utility rooms — the wider light spread avoids a flat, dark feel, and the trade-off of slightly more glare is low-risk in spaces where people aren't seated for long.
Do downlights need to be dimmable? ▼
Not essential, but strongly recommended for living areas and any space using wide-beam or frosted fixtures — dimming lets you manage the brightness trade-offs that come with those choices. Check your dimmer is LED-compatible and matched to your fixture's minimum load.
Do I need to plan downlights differently in a room with a ceiling fan? ▼
Yes, in two ways. First, placement: keep fixtures clear of the fan's full blade sweep radius plus a safety margin, not just clear of the motor housing — this usually pushes downlights outward toward the walls rather than clustering near the fan. The safe margin isn't fixed: it depends on beam angle and how far below the ceiling the fan hangs, so a fan on a raked ceiling or long downrod needs more clearance than a standard-mount fan. Use the Ceiling Fan & Downlight Clearance visualizer to check an exact layout in 3D. Second, fixture type: avoid frosted/opal diffuser downlights in a room where the fan and lights run at the same time — a blade sweeping in front of a wide, diffuse light source causes a visible strobe/flicker that a narrower anti-glare or standard reflector fixture doesn't produce nearly as badly. Frosted downlights are really only safe with a ceiling fan in a room where the two are reliably never on together, which in practice is usually just a main/master bedroom.
